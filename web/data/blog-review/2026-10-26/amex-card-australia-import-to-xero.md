## Content

<p>If you run a business in Australia on an American Express card, learning to <strong>import AMEX Card Australia statement PDF to Xero</strong> is the fastest way to turn a monthly card statement into reconciled books. The statement itself is a PDF full of charges, payments, credits, and fees, and retyping it by hand is slow, dull, and easy to get wrong. A clean CSV import, done once a month, gives you categorized expenses, a card balance you can trust, and a paper trail that holds up at tax time.</p>
<p>This guide walks through the whole route for Australian cardholders: downloading the right statement from your Amex account, converting it to a Xero-ready CSV, mapping the fields so Xero reads every row correctly, handling GST the way the Australian Taxation Office expects, and reconciling the card account so nothing is counted twice. It also covers the traps that catch Australian users specifically, from foreign currency charges to supplementary cards held by staff.</p>
<h2>Download the right AMEX Card Australia statement</h2>
<p>Start in your American Express online account, not in a spending summary or an app activity feed. Open the Australian card you want to import, choose the billing period, and download the official monthly statement as a PDF. The statement is the document of record: it carries the opening balance, the closing balance, every charge and payment in the period, interest, and fees. An activity view may look similar, but it can miss adjustments that appear only on the statement, and it does not give you the closing balance you need to reconcile against later.</p>
<p>If you hold more than one Australian Amex card, download each card separately. One card per file and one Xero credit card account per card is the rule that keeps everything else simple. Business owners should also check whether staff supplementary cards are included on the same statement, as they usually are for Australian corporate and business cards. Keep the full statement intact for conversion and sort out who spent what during the cleanup pass, while the purchases are still fresh. Our <a href="/banks/amex-card-australia">AMEX Card Australia conversion guide</a> explains the statement layout in more detail.</p>
<h2>Convert the PDF to a Xero-ready CSV</h2>
<p>Upload the PDF to a statement converter and choose CSV output. A good converter reads the statement table and rebuilds it as rows with Date, Description, and Amount columns. Before you download, check the preview carefully. Every charge should be one row, payments should carry the opposite sign to charges, and the row count should roughly match the number of transactions printed on the statement. If a row looks split across two lines, fix it now, because a half-row in Xero becomes a mystery expense later.</p>
<p>Three details matter most for Australian cards. First, keep one date format for the whole file, because Xero expects consistency, and Australian statements use day-month-year order. Second, keep amounts in Australian dollars only. Amex statements show foreign currency charges with both the original amount and the converted AUD amount, and Xero needs the AUD figure that actually affects your balance. Third, strip currency symbols from the amount column so Xero reads the values as numbers. Our <a href="/blog/amex-convert-to-csv-quickbooks">Amex conversion guide</a> walks through the same cleanup for QuickBooks users, and the principles are identical.</p>

<h2>Map the fields for a clean Xero import</h2>
<p>In Xero, open the credit card account for this Amex card, then choose to import a bank statement and upload your CSV. Xero asks you to map each column: Date to the transaction date, Description to the payee or description field, and Amount to the amount. Take a moment on this screen, because a mapping mistake here repeats on every row. The first row of the preview should be a real charge, not a header line, and the last row should be a real transaction, not a statement footer.</p>
<p>Xero also asks about the account type and currency. Set the account as a credit card account in AUD, and confirm the statement period matches the file you are importing. Import one billing period at a time, oldest to newest, so the running balance in Xero moves in the same order as the statement. If you import periods out of order, the balance will still end up right, but tracking down a gap becomes much harder. When the import finishes, Xero places the rows in the bank reconciliation screen, ready for matching and coding.</p>
<h2>Handle GST, supplementary cards, and foreign charges</h2>
<p>Australian GST handling is where a careless import costs real money. Not every charge on an Amex statement includes GST, and not every charge that includes it is fully claimable. Card fees, interest, and overseas purchases generally carry no GST, while most domestic business purchases do. During the coding pass, assign GST-inclusive tax rates only to purchases where GST was actually charged, and use a no-GST or exempt code for fees, interest, and international charges. Guessing here inflates your BAS claim, which is exactly the kind of error that draws attention at audit time.</p>
<p>Supplementary cards deserve their own pass. Tag each charge by cardholder so staff spending reports itself, and check that personal spending on a business card is separated out rather than coded as a business expense. Foreign currency charges need a second look too: import the AUD converted amount, and if Xero shows a small exchange difference against a bill already entered in the original currency, post the difference to a currency gain or loss account rather than forcing the numbers to match. Keep the statement itself filed with the month, because it is the source document for every row you just imported.</p>

<h2>Reconcile in Xero and avoid double-counted spending</h2>
<p>Reconciliation is the step that proves the import worked. In Xero, reconcile the credit card account against the statement: the Xero balance after coding must equal the statement closing balance. Work through the imported rows methodically, matching charges to bills and receipts, coding the rest, and creating bank rules for recurring vendors such as fuel, software, and office suppliers. Bank rules turn next month's import into a ten-minute job instead of an afternoon.</p>
<p>The two classic errors are easy to name and easy to avoid. First, never record the payment you make from your bank account as a new expense: the purchases were the expenses, and the payment is a transfer from your bank account to the card account. Recording it as spending double-counts the whole month. Second, pick one import path per card account, file or bank feed, and stick to it. Importing the same period twice, once from each path, creates duplicates that look exactly like real spending. <h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<p><strong>Can I import an AMEX Card Australia statement PDF directly into Xero?</strong><br />Xero imports CSV files, not PDFs, so convert the statement PDF to a CSV first, then import that file into the credit card account for the card. The conversion takes a few minutes and preserves every transaction row.</p>
<p><strong>How do I handle GST on Amex charges in Xero?</strong><br />Code GST-inclusive tax rates only on domestic purchases where GST was charged. Use a no-GST code for card fees, interest, and overseas purchases, which generally carry no GST, and keep the statement filed as the source document.</p>
<p><strong>What about charges in foreign currency?</strong><br />Import the AUD converted amount shown on the statement. The original foreign amount is useful context, but Xero needs the AUD figure that affects your card balance, with any small difference posted to currency gain or loss.</p>
<p><strong>Should the card payment from my bank account be an expense?</strong><br />No. Record it as a transfer from your bank account to the card account. The purchases themselves were the expenses, and recording the payment as spending would count everything twice.</p>
